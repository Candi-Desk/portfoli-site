Commercial mandate · Emirates Post Group · 2021–2023

Moving a nation’s post office online

Director of Product Development & Pricing, with full P&L ownership of the retail portfolio. Renewals went from 5% to 70% online and two decades of decline reversed from −15% to −3% a year.

An employed mandate with a profit and loss attached. Director of Product Development & Pricing, accountable for the retail portfolio of the UAE's national postal operator. Every figure below was reported internally against budget.

Role

Director, Product Development & Pricing

Scope

Retail portfolio, full P&L, about €240M

Stakeholders

Board, chairman’s office, IT, operations, legal, sister companies, consultants

Timeline

Two and a half years

01 — The problem

A queue that arrived every January and damaged everything else

P.O. Box rental renews annually, and in the United Arab Emirates almost everybody renewed in the first quarter, in person. Branches built for parcels, shipping and counter services spent the opening weeks of each year processing a transaction that required no human judgement at all.

The cost was not the renewal queue. It was everything standing behind it. Service quality for shipping and other revenue-generating services degraded for weeks because capacity had been consumed by an administrative task.

Underneath sat a longer problem. The retail portfolio had been shrinking by 15 to 20% a year for two decades — the structural decline of physical mail, which no amount of operational efficiency reverses.

02 — How I framed it

Not a capacity problem — a reason-to-queue problem

The obvious responses were operational: more counters, extended hours, appointment slots. All of them accept the queue and manage it better, and all of them cost money every January in perpetuity.

The customer was not attached to the branch. They were attached to a renewal that had only ever been possible in one place. The online channel existed and carried about 5% of renewals, which told me the barrier was not capability but the absence of any reason to change behaviour.

That reframing moved the problem out of operations and into product and pricing, where I could actually act on it.

03 — What I did

Make the digital path cheaper, easier and better than the counter

Three levers, applied together rather than sequentially, because any one of them alone leaves a reason to keep queuing.

  • A pricing incentive that made the digital renewal the economically rational choice, not merely the convenient one.
  • A simplified sign-up, removing the friction that sent people back to a counter where a human would handle it for them.
  • Campaigns timed to the renewal cycle, so the message arrived when the customer was already thinking about it.

The same approach carried the Digital Mailbox, which upgraded physical box holders to a digital mailbox with home delivery — a product change, not a channel change, and a materially better proposition for anyone who had been collecting post in person out of habit.

The decline did not stop — a structurally shrinking category does not reverse in two years — but it was slowed to a fifth of its historical rate while revenue rose materially from the second year. Alongside the renewal work, a new product idea, a crypto stamp, generated over €1 million in a single campaign, which demonstrated that the customer base had appetite for products nobody had previously offered it.

05 — The harder problem

A group competing with itself

Successive restructurings had left the post office and its sister companies — notably the courier arm — selling overlapping products to the same customers. In the government sector this was acute: one client, several account contacts, unclear ownership, and service failures that were politically sensitive in a way commercial accounts are not.

External consultants had worked the problem for months without resolving it.

I produced a customer and product segmentation strategy for the group, with bundled industry solutions aimed at the government sector, in one week. It was approved and praised by senior management and the chairman’s office.

The speed was not brilliance. It was proximity. I had spent two years inside the pricing and product data; consultants were reconstructing from the outside what I could already see. The transferable lesson is about where a problem is best solved, not how fast.

That segmentation work fed a group-level cost-based pricing project, run with the printing and courier companies, which reset P.O. Box rental and renewal pricing and retail service pricing while removing the overlaps that had caused the conflict.

06 — What was hard

Every lever belonged to somebody else

I owned the P&L. I did not own the branch network, the IT roadmap, the courier company, or the pricing of the services my own portfolio was bundled with.

A channel migration touches all of them, and a pricing incentive that moves customers away from branches is a proposal that branch operations can reasonably resist. Most of the work was not analysis. It was assembling the evidence that made the case unarguable, then taking it to the board, to operations and to the sister companies in the terms each of them cared about: service quality, cost per transaction, and revenue protection.

What I would do differently

I would instrument the channel shift from the first week rather than reading it quarterly. We knew the direction of travel but could not see weekly movement, which meant campaign timing was argued from judgement rather than from data. It is exactly the gap I closed later, with far smaller stakes, on Trip’in.

07 — Why this case is here

The same claim, at national scale

Building products people adopt is easy to assert and hard to evidence. This is the clearest evidence I have: a behaviour that had not changed in decades, changed deliberately, measured, and sustained — in a regulated, politically sensitive, state-owned environment where the easy options were all operational.
