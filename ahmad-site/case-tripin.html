Trip’in

A zero-login intake tool that replaces the group chat where travel plans go to die. 45.45% intake conversion against a sector benchmark near 3.5%.

A programme project, not a client mandate. It was run solo as part of the Digital Product Management programme at Franklin Institute Berlin, and it is graded rather than revenue-bearing. The commercial track record sits on the home page.

Role

Sole product manager, researcher and builder

Timeline

Two cycles, 06/2026 – 07/2026

Stack

Relume, Webflow, Typeform, Google Sheets, GA4, Jira, Figma

Graded

01 — The problem

Planning a trip together is an administrative job nobody volunteered for

Organising a group trip starts with optimism and a new chat thread. Within a day the thread holds forty messages, three proposed dates, two half-answered questions about money, and one person — the organiser — scrolling upward trying to reconcile all of it by hand.

Two failures compound. The first is administrative: preferences arrive as unstructured text, so the organiser becomes a human database, manually balancing schedules and budgets that were never captured in a comparable form. The second is social, and it is the one that actually freezes the plan. People will not state a budget in front of their friends. The number they can afford and the number they are willing to type into a group chat are different numbers.

So the planning loop stalls, and the trip quietly does not happen.

02 — Research

Seven interviews, and one finding that changed the product

I ran seven structured one-to-one interviews with people who plan group trips regularly, and synthesised three personas from them. The organiser persona — a frequent planner, four to six trips a year — described the same friction every time: the thread goes silent the moment prices enter the conversation.

“I just want a clear overview of what my friends actually want to do without having to chase them down one by one over text.”

The finding I had not expected was unanimous. Every participant confirmed budget stigma: social pressure causes people to hide their real financial constraints inside a group setting. That is not a usability problem, and no amount of interface polish solves it.

It does, however, have a product answer. If the constraint is being seen, remove the audience. A blinded intake — where each participant submits privately and only the aggregate is shown — lets people be honest because nobody is watching. The research did not refine the design; it determined it.

03 — Market position

Competing with a WhatsApp poll, not with booking platforms

A Porter’s Five Forces assessment made the threat obvious: generic form builders have almost no barrier to entry, so defensibility could not come from the form itself. It had to come from travel-specific logic — structured variables tuned to pre-booking group alignment, which a general-purpose builder has no reason to add.

The more useful question was the next best alternative. Trip’in does not compete with online travel agencies; those solve booking, which happens later. It competes with the static WhatsApp poll — instant, free, and buried within an hour. The positioning follows from that: the deployment speed of a shared link, with the back-end integrity of a structured database.

04 — The decision

No accounts, and no honest answers without them

The hard trade-off was authentication. Requiring sign-up would have given me identity, persistence, notifications and a basis for retention — everything a real product eventually needs.

It would also have killed the thing I was testing. The organiser shares one link into an existing group chat; every account wall between that link and a submitted preference is a place to drop out. And I was not trying to validate retention. I was trying to validate whether people would tell the truth about money if nobody could see them.

Zero-login, by deliberate choice, with the cost accepted: no identity, no returning users, no notification channel. Those are version two problems. Honest data was the version one question.

05 — What I built

A no-code stack, chosen for sprint velocity

The prototype avoids back-end engineering entirely, so the first version could reach real users within the sprint rather than after it.

  • Relume — AI-assisted responsive sitemap and wireframes, mapping layout elements straight to target user goals.
  • Webflow — the front end: a semantic staging hub with one high-contrast call to action above the fold.
  • Typeform — the intake engine, with conditional logic branching so the questions isolate parameters per journey type.
  • Google Sheets — a flat-file back end, readable and shareable without building a database.
  • GA4 — custom telemetry focused on top-of-funnel velocity.
  • Jira — five epics, run as a sprint from infrastructure setup through to final performance analysis.

Observational testing identified the moment users recognised the value: when the questions adapted in real time to the journey they had selected. Not the landing page, not the branding — the point where the tool visibly understood what kind of trip they were planning.

Stripping away registration is what produced the conversion figure; the engagement time shows the value was understood before the click rather than after it. The qualitative data held up too: one submission arrived as a fully-formed, multilingual trip proposal written straight into the back end, which told me the open field had become a promotional canvas rather than a chore — once the placeholder text guided people to use it that way.

07 — What went wrong

The flatline at exactly ten

During acquisition monitoring, GA4 showed click traffic climbing on the landing page while incoming database rows stopped dead. Not tapering — stopped, at exactly ten records.

For a few hours the data read like a conversion collapse: people arriving, engaging, and abandoning at the form. The real cause was infrastructure. The form vendor’s free tier caps submissions at ten per month. Users were completing the flow; the system was refusing to record them.

The learning

Validate the infrastructure ceiling before driving traffic, and instrument the entire funnel rather than the landing page alone. A vendor limit and user friction produce the same shape at the top of the funnel and opposite conclusions at the bottom. The instrumentation is the only reason this surfaced during the campaign instead of in the post-mortem — and it is why the rollout was paused rather than scaled into a broken pipeline.

It is also a reminder that a prototype stack is a set of borrowed constraints. Choosing no-code buys speed and inherits somebody else’s limits, which have to be read before they are hit.

08 — Second cycle

Reframing from clicks to cognitive load

The second cycle asked a different question: not whether people convert, but what the tool costs them mentally. Jobs to be Done interviews and a journey analysis located anxiety spikes in the gather and confirm phases — precisely where data is fragmented across sources and the organiser has to hold it all in their head.

Formal audits followed: Nielsen’s heuristics against the interface, and a Gestalt review of proximity, similarity and simplicity measured against the alternatives people actually use. The competitor matrix made the structural point — manual tools offer flexibility and near-zero decision support, so the user maintains system status by hand across several tabs, which is the real source of planning fatigue.

The output was a research report opening with an executive summary in Situation–Complication–Resolution form, four named barriers to sustained use, and a centralised coordination dashboard prototyped in Figma, with every interface element mapped to a specific job to be done.

09 — What I would do next

Three things, in order

  • Remove the ceiling. Fund the form tier or replace it — the constraint is now known, measured and cheap to solve.
  • Dual-input cost controls. Testers valued the descriptive budget tiers but asked for absolute figures alongside them. Both, not one.
  • Close the loop. Automated outbound link generation and an aggregated summary dashboard, so the organiser moves from collecting answers to acting on them.

Retention is the question I have not answered. A zero-login tool cannot bring anyone back, and a planning tool used once a quarter needs a reason to be remembered. That is the next real product problem, and it is bigger than any feature on this list.
