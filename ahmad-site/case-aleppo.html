Programme practice · Design Thinking project · 2026

Aleppo Civic Connect

A civic reporting tool for municipal maintenance in a city with almost no resources left. The design question was not how to collect more complaints, but how to make the existing ones actionable.

A programme project, not a client mandate. It was run solo as part of the Digital Product Management programme at Franklin Institute Berlin, and it is graded rather than revenue-bearing. The commercial track record sits on the home page.

Role

Sole researcher and designer

Timeline

26/05 – 02/06/2026

Methods

Background research, user interviews, affinity and empathy mapping, How Might We, lo-fi prototype, usability test

Graded

92/100

01 — The context

The scarcity is physical; the abundance is digital

Western Aleppo after the conflict presents an unusual pairing of numbers. The municipality has lost roughly 78% of its functional waste-management vehicles, transit fuel and workforce. At the same time 82% of residents still use mobile data to follow what is happening in their city.

Between those two facts sits the failure. 94% of public complaints are trapped in un-indexed social media groups — visible to neighbours, invisible to anyone who could act. Nothing about that data is structured, prioritised, or addressed to the department that owns the problem.

Two consequences follow. Citizens bundle national utility crises with municipal duties, so the complaints that do reach officials are jammed with items no municipality can act on. And with no communication from leadership, families rely on rumour, which converts ordinary frustration into misdirected anger.

02 — Research

Two groups, one broken contract

Background research across public sources was followed by user interviews with both sides of the exchange: residents reporting problems, and the officials who are supposed to resolve them. Findings were synthesised into affinity and empathy maps, then framed as How Might We statements.

The interviews made the shape of the problem clear. Residents did not lack a way to complain — they lacked any evidence that complaining achieved anything. Officials did not lack reports; they lacked a way to tell which ones were real, local, and within their mandate.

Both groups were acting reasonably, and the result was mutual distrust. That is a civic trust problem wearing the costume of a workflow problem.

03 — The decision

Three districts, not a city

The instinct with civic technology is to design for the whole population. With 78% of operational capacity missing, that guarantees failure: reports arrive, nothing can be fixed, and the tool proves the very distrust it was built to repair.

I narrowed the deployment to three districts with intact street topography — Al-Hamadaniyah, Al-Mogambo and Al-Sariyan — where rapid maintenance loops are physically possible. The aim was early operational wins that are visible to residents, rather than complete coverage that cannot be serviced.

It is a product decision, not a technical one. In an environment with no slack, a narrow scope that works beats a broad scope that disappoints, and the trust earned in three districts is the asset that funds the fourth.

04 — The concept

A mutual utility engine

The design pairs two loops that only work together.

  • Citizen accountability. A fast, lightweight portal for logging a local incident, plus a live dual-mood tracking widget that maps public sentiment — turning a complaint into constructive feedback rather than an outburst into a void.
  • Official prioritisation. Structured, geospatial asset metrics for field engineers, and an objective prioritisation queue that justifies where scarce resources go and shields field workers from the accusation of favouritism.

The connecting measure is the one that matters: a transparent operational benchmark mapping citizen mood against official completion rates. Each side can see what the other is doing. That is the mechanism by which trust is rebuilt, and it is the product, not a feature of it.

05 — What went wrong

I designed for the citizen and nearly lost the official

The first concept was a reporting app. It was faster, cleaner and better designed for the resident than what shipped — and it would have failed.

It would have increased the volume of incoming reports against a municipality with no capacity to serve them, making the backlog visible without making it smaller. The official side of the interviews is what caught it: their problem was never a shortage of reports.

The learning

In a two-sided system, the side that is easier to empathise with is the side you will accidentally optimise for. Residents are sympathetic and easy to imagine; the overwhelmed official is neither. Designing only for the sympathetic user produces a tool that increases load on the constrained side and collapses. I now treat the constrained side as the primary design constraint, not the secondary audience.

06 — What I would do next

Prove the loop before adding to it

  • Test the benchmark with officials first. Public completion rates are an accountability mechanism pointed at the municipality, and it will be resisted. If it cannot survive that conversation, the trust loop does not close and the rest is decoration.
  • Validate the mood widget against behaviour, not against stated sentiment. People report differently when they believe someone is listening.
  • Define the handover to field engineers properly. The prioritisation queue is only useful if it survives contact with how crews are actually dispatched.

This project remains a concept, tested with users but never deployed. I am clear about that. What it demonstrates is the method and the judgement about scope — not an outcome in the field.
